### 2.1 The Spatial Revolution: LiDAR and Low-Density Urbanism
Prior to the deployment of Airborne Laser Scanning (LiDAR) by Damian Evans and the Khmer Archaeology LiDAR Consortium (KALC) in 2012 and 2015, Angkor was primarily studied through its monumental stone temples (Angkor Wat, the Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, Baphuon). Modern scholarship now understands these stone edifices as mere civic-religious nodal anchors within an immense, contiguous, low-density peri-urban agrarian matrix:
* **The Urban Footprint:** Spanned more than **1,000 square kilometers** (comparable in territorial footprint to modern Los Angeles or Greater London).
* **Grid Settlement Morphology:** LiDAR penetrations through the tropical jungle canopy revealed tens of thousands of previously invisible domestic earthen occupation mounds (*knor*), house mounds, local communal ponds (*trapeang*), and meticulously gridded road networks connecting farmers to central markets.
* **The Population Dynamic:** Demographic modeling based on mound counts and rice consumption estimates places Greater Angkor's population at **750,000 to 900,000 people** at its zenith in the late 12th to early 13th century under Jayavarman VII.

### 2.2 The Mechanics of the "Hydraulic Machine"
Angkor was built on a very gentle, continuous slope (roughly 1 meter drop per kilometer) tilting from the sandstone massif of Mount Kulen in the northeast down toward the Great Lake (Tonle Sap) in the southwest. The Khmer hydraulic engineers harnessed this topography to create an unprecedented gravity-fed water-control system:

#### 1. The Barays (Elevated Reservoirs)
Unlike modern reservoirs that are excavated into the ground, Angkor's *barays* were constructed by building colossal earthen dikes **above the natural ground level**:
* **The West Baray (*Baray Techo*):** Engineered in the 11th century under Suryavarman I and Udayadityavarman II. Dimensions: 8 kilometers long by 2.1 kilometers wide, with dikes reaching 10–12 meters in height. Stored an estimated **120,000,000 to 140,000,000 cubic meters** of water.
* **The East Baray (*Yashodharatataka*):** Built c. 900 CE under Yasovarman I. Dimensions: 7.1 kilometers by 1.7 kilometers; stored approximately 110,000,000 cubic meters.
* **Jayatataka (North Baray):** Engineered in the late 12th century under Jayavarman VII, surrounding the Neak Pean temple.

#### 2. Gravity-Fed Sluices and Distribution Canals
Because the reservoirs were elevated above the plain, water did not require mechanical lifting. It was discharged through stone and wooden masonry sluice gates (*khpob*) into a vast labyrinth of secondary canals, distributary ditches, and gridded rice paddies.

#### 3. Dual-Purpose Hydrology: Flood Attenuation vs. Dry-Season Irrigation
* **Wet-Season Defense (June–October):** During the ferocious Southwest Monsoon, the canals and barays acted as giant retention basins, mitigating devastating flash floods pouring off Mount Kulen and preventing the drowning of low-lying settlements.
* **Dry-Season Production (November–May):** During the parched Northeast Monsoon, stored water was released into the paddy networks, enabling a second (and in some areas, third) annual crop of flood-retreat wet rice, multiplying agricultural output well beyond natural climatic limits.

### 3.1 The Dendrochronological Evidence: *Fokienia hodginsii*
The critical empirical breakthrough linking Angkor's collapse to climate volatility was executed by **Dr. Brendan Buckley** and the Tree-Ring Laboratory at Columbia University's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, publishing in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS)* in 2010.

* **The Proxy:** Buckley sampled long-lived cypress trees (*Fokienia hodginsii*, known locally as *Po Mu*) in the misty Bidoup Nui Ba National Park in the Central Highlands of Vietnam, roughly 400 kilometers east of Angkor. These trees live for over 1,000 years and are extraordinarily sensitive to annual soil moisture fluctuations driven by the East Asian and Southwest Asian monsoon systems.
* **The Monsoon Asian Drought Atlas (MADA):** By cross-referencing narrow tree-growth rings with historical precipitation indices, Buckley established an annual **Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI)** spanning 1250 to 2008 CE.

### 3.2 The Two Megadroughts and the Deadly Deluge
The dendrochronological data revealed that the 14th and 15th centuries were the most climatically anomalous centuries in Southeast Asia in over a millennium:
1. **Megadrought 1 (c. 1345–1374 CE):** Spanned nearly three decades. Consecutive years exhibited the narrowest tree rings in the 759-year record. Inscriptions at Angkor ceased entirely; stone construction halted; reservoirs dried to muddy basins.
2. **The Sudden Deluge Shock (c. 1375–1385 CE):** The megadrought did not conclude with mild rains. The climate flipped violently from hyper-arid El Niño-like patterns into super-monsoon La Niña deluges. In multiple single seasons, precipitation exceeded historical averages by over 150%, sending catastrophic runoff surges into an unmaintained, heavily silted canal network.
3. **Megadrought 2 (c. 1401–1425 CE):** A second severe drought struck early in the 15th century, suffocating the empire's remaining attempts to reconstruct its shattered waterways.
